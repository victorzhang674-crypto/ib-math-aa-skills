# Plan and write an AA mathematical exploration

## Shape the question

- Ask the student what they want to find out and which mathematics they can explain. Compare possible topics by the clarity of the result, the level of mathematics, the availability of evidence and the chance to make an independent decision. Recommend a focused question with a realistic pilot and an analytic fallback if needed.
- Define the object, variables or parameters, units, domain and intended comparison or result. For modeling, identify the observations, how they can be obtained or cited, and a way to check the model. For a theoretical inquiry, start with small cases, formulate a conjecture and identify how it could be justified or disproved.
- Run a feasibility check. Try one calculation, one limiting case, or a small set of observations. State what result would prompt a narrower scope or a different method. Match the depth to SL or HL and build complexity only where the question requires it.

## Turn the idea into a writing plan

| Section | Evidence the student should produce |
| --- | --- |
| **Introduction and aim** | A concise statement of the investigation, scope, and the outcome that would answer it. Explain the student's reason for choosing this question where it bears on a decision. |
| **Setup** | Definitions, assumptions, units and boundaries. Document the source or collection method for data. Explain why the chosen mathematics fits the aim and what it assumes. |
| **Development** | Derive or motivate the model or key result. Show representative calculations, proofs, or sufficient detail about a computational method to reproduce the result. Put figures and tables alongside the steps they support. |
| **Check and refine** | Compare the result against relevant evidence, an independent calculation, a special case, an alternative method or a sensitivity test. Interpret what the check reveals and refine the approach when warranted. |
| **Conclusion and sources** | Answer the aim within the supported domain, with the relevant quantities and qualifications. Explain which limitation most affects the conclusion and give a specific extension if it follows from the work. Cite sources where they are used and include a bibliography. |

Structure is flexible. Interleave evaluation with mathematical development whenever a check changes the next step. For graphs, state the observation and explain the inference it supports. For technology, explain the inputs and procedure and give a mathematical check on consequential outputs. For proofs, explain why the steps cover the intended cases.

If the user asks for a schedule, map the remaining time to tangible checkpoints. Start with a topic and pilot, then a validated mathematical core, a complete draft, and revision based on feedback. Identify what the student should bring to the teacher at the next checkpoint, such as a graph with a suspicious residual pattern or a conjecture that fails at a boundary.

## Two ways to turn a topic into a plan

- **Optimization.** For “How does a fixed allowance for seams change the dimensions that minimize the material used for a cylindrical container of fixed volume?”, define the geometry and allowance convention, express the objective in one variable, and determine feasible dimensions. Check that the stationary point is a minimum over the feasible domain. Compare the optimal dimensions under zero and nonzero allowance and test how the result changes with the assumed allowance.
- **Theoretical investigation.** For “Which axis-aligned rectangles with positive integer sides and a fixed integer perimeter have the largest area?”, compute small cases, formulate the conjecture in terms of the semiperimeter, then use a completed square or a discrete argument to handle odd and even semiperimeters. Ask the student to check edge cases and judge whether extending the restriction changes the result. This example illustrates the workflow. Evaluate the depth of any proposed extension against the student's level before recommending the topic.
